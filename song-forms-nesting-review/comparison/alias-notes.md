# Catalog alias review

Reviewed all 364 catalog rows. Of these, 177 have an “Also called” field with content and 187 have no aliases. The table headings, examples, count summary, and proposed section types are outside that denominator. 75 overrides: A = 21, B = 27, C = 6, D = 14, E = 7. The original Markdown retains the complete source wording.

**How to interpret an override.** Keys are exact source titles. Each reason cites the source row and identifies the problem. The revised string supplies alternate names or conventional search notations for this title. An en dash means no reliable equivalent remains in the supplied field. Excluded items may be valuable examples, narrower forms, related forms, or techniques. Do not promote them to new entries or add relationship edges by string matching. Sources in the records supplement the local catalog when needed; an empty reference list means the distinction was made against the existing entries. Retained material is not an independent scholarly endorsement of every synonym.

**Notation.** Retain useful conventional patterns when the surrounding title makes the object clear. AABABA describes the documented extension; “compound AABA” refers to hierarchy inside the sections. ABAB alone fails to distinguish simple from contrasting verse–chorus. Bar counts, rhyme schemes, metrical notation and word counts are different kinds of data even when written with similar letters or numerals.

**Collisions worth retaining as research candidates.** “Tag” alone can denote several kinds of ending beyond a barbershop tag. “Kaiso” and “calypso,” “cornkister” and “bothy ballad,” and “capstan shanty” and “windlass shanty” have regional, period or work-context questions. “Stornello” and “ritornello” require the Italian vernacular context and must not imply that an instrumental ritornello is a stornello. “Open field,” “open form,” “American popular song form,” and “Tin Pan Alley form” can function as shorthand in narrower contexts; they are not sufficient evidence to merge any two entries. “English dactylic hexameter” fits this entry’s Evangeline example; the possible mismatch between its broader heading and narrower example is a definition-scope question. “Ruba’i” remains available for the singular quatrain in “Rubaiyat stanza”; it must not be expanded to imply a complete chained sequence. “Riding rhyme,” “Sicilian octave/strambotto,” “Old English line,” and similar historical terms also depend on period or language. Original evidence is retained even when an unqualified mapping is excluded from the alias override. No new synonym or new catalog row was inferred from those candidates.

**Identity-sensitive examples.** A head arrangement concerns how an arrangement was developed or remembered. An answer song can answer an entirely different recording. A Victorian part-song is one historical use of the broader term. Englyn variants, distinct Irish meters and the kasen layout remain within their original entries unless independently authorized as catalog additions. Names of canticles, doxologies, counting songs and particular works supply repertoire examples.
